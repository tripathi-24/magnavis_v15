		\textbf{Thesis Title}: Magnetic signature analysis and Anomaly Detection\\
		\textbf{Thesis Supervisor}: Prof. Saikat Ghosh and Prof. Amitangshu Pal \\
		\textbf{Month and Year of Thesis Submission}: June 2026\\[0.1cm] }
		\HRule \\[0.1cm] 	
Magnetic field measurements provide a sensitive window into both natural and human-made processes in the environment. Variations in the geomagnetic field arise from solar-terrestrial interactions, ionospheric currents, and local geology, while man-made sources such as electric machinery, vehicles, power lines etc introduce short-lived disturbances. Because magnetic signals integrate over space and respond quickly to changes, long-term magnetic monitoring is widely used in geophysics, environmental monitoring, and infrastructure assessment. However, the same sensitivity that makes magnetic measurements valuable also makes them noisy: transient disturbances and site-specific effects can mask the baseline signal and bias any downstream analysis.

This research is a practical, real-world monitoring setup: two observatories operating in parallel, each equipped with three sensors, continuously recording magnetic data. The primary goal is to study, analyse, and visualise the measurements, and then build a robust anomaly detection system that removes spurious measurements to obtain a clean baseline. The clean baseline is then used to train a model for detecting anthropogenic disturbances in the environment by correlating magnetic signals across both observatories. The dual-observatory design is critical because it allows differentiation between local noise (for example, observatory-specific electrical equipment) and broader disturbances that appear in both locations.

The approach of using multiple sensors per site aligns with established practices in redundancy and noise rejection. Multiple co-located sensors enable cross-checking, identification of instrument drift, and reduction of random noise through aggregation, which improves the reliability of the measured baseline.

Magnetic anomaly detection is commonly approached through statistical thresholds, signal processing, or machine learning. Classical statistical methods estimate a baseline from historical data and flag deviations that exceed a threshold defined by the mean and standard deviation of prediction errors. Recent work has shown that recurrent neural networks can learn smooth diurnal variation and that anomalies are often detected more reliably from prediction residuals than from raw magnitude alone.

This thesis implements and evaluates Magnavis, a software pipeline for two parallel observatories (OBS-1 and OBS-2), each with three co-located sensors. Measured resultant magnetic field is compared with one-step-ahead forecasts from pretrained gated recurrent unit (GRU) and long short-term memory (LSTM) models. An exponential moving average is applied to the absolute residual; a second is flagged anomalous when the residual exceeds the running mean plus $k$ times the running standard deviation, where $k$ is a tunable multiplier. A reproducible offline benchmark on manually labelled CSV data compares statistical smoothers, a session-trained attention bidirectional LSTM, and the pretrained recurrent models under a zero-historic, predict-only protocol.

On short-duration ground truth (sparse anomalies), several schemes achieve high recall at moderate $k$. On long-duration ground truth (sustained anthropogenic offsets), simple smoothers applied directly to the raw field fail at $k \ge 2$, whereas neural predictors remain useful. Pretrained LSTM maintains approximately 85\% point-level recall for $k$ from 2 to 5 on long campaigns; pretrained GRU matches LSTM at $k=3$ but falls to about 63\% for $k \ge 4$ because the model tracks sustained plateaus and interior residuals shrink. Closed-loop autoregressive rolling does not remove this gap. The study recommends LSTM at $k \approx 3$ for long monitoring periods and documents the software artefacts needed to reproduce the results. Where tri-axial components are available, perturbation-vector direction and two-station triangulation provide a geometric characterisation of events, with limitations discussed in the concluding chapter.
